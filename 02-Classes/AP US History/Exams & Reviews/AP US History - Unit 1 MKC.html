AP US History — Unit 1 MKC (1491–1607)

← Exams & Reviews · Study Guide · Concept Map

Key concepts

  • KC 1.1: Native peoples developed diverse and complex societies by adapting to and changing different North American environments.
  • KC 1.2: Contact among Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans created the Columbian Exchange and major demographic, economic, social, political, and cultural changes.
  • KC 2.1 connection: European powers developed different colonial systems based on their goals, cultures, environments, and relationships with Native peoples.

Indigenous societies before European contact

Question of the day: How did Indigenous nations differ from European societies?

MKC term or date What it means How it answers the question
60,000–14,000 years ago The broad era during which the ancestors of Native Americans migrated from Asia into the Americas, including movement across Beringia during the Ice Age. Over thousands of years, their descendants spread throughout the continents. Long separation and adaptation to many environments produced numerous independent nations rather than one shared “Native American” society.
Three Sisters: maize, beans, and squash Crops planted together because corn supported beans, beans enriched the soil, and squash preserved moisture and discouraged weeds. This system supplied a balanced diet and reliable harvests. Indigenous peoples used sophisticated agricultural knowledge suited to their environments; this farming supported settled communities and larger populations.
Aztecs A powerful tribute empire centered at Tenochtitlan in central Mexico. It had cities, markets, social classes, religious institutions, and intensive agriculture. The Aztecs show that the Americas contained large, politically complex urban civilizations, unlike the European stereotype of an undeveloped wilderness.
Mississippi Mound Builders Agricultural societies that built large ceremonial and burial mounds in the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys. Cahokia was a major urban and trade center near present-day St. Louis. Fertile river valleys supported dense populations, specialized labor, trade networks, and hierarchical government.
Western Indigenous nations Societies varied by environment: Pueblo peoples used irrigation and adobe towns in the Southwest, while many Great Basin and Plains peoples moved seasonally to obtain scarce resources. Their different economies and settlement patterns demonstrate how climate, water, plants, and animals shaped Native life.
East Coast Indigenous nations Many northeastern and southeastern peoples combined maize agriculture with hunting, fishing, and trade. Groups such as the Powhatan lived in villages organized into regional political alliances. Compared with Europeans, they often organized land use, political authority, and community relationships through kinship and collective traditions.
Great League of Peace The Haudenosaunee, or Iroquois Confederacy, united the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca nations; the Tuscarora joined later. Its council helped member nations cooperate while retaining local identities. It demonstrates sophisticated Indigenous diplomacy and confederated government rather than centralized monarchy in the European style.
Native American religion Beliefs differed among nations but often treated spiritual power as present in animals, plants, places, and natural forces. Religious life was commonly woven into community practices rather than separated into a single institution. Many Europeans promoted exclusive Christianity, while Native spiritual traditions often emphasized relationships among people, nature, and the spirit world.
Native American gender roles Roles differed by nation, but women often farmed, controlled household resources, or influenced kinship and political decisions; men often hunted, traded, and fought. Some societies were matrilineal. European societies generally gave married women fewer property and political rights, so Native gender and family structures could appear unfamiliar to Europeans.

Nice to know

Term Short description How it applies
Cahokia The largest Mississippian center, with monumental mounds and extensive trade networks. Evidence of urban development and complex social organization before contact.
Pueblo irrigation Canals and water-management techniques supported farming in the dry Southwest. A clear example of adapting to and transforming an environment.
Matrilineal kinship Family identity, inheritance, or clan membership passed through the mother's line in some Native societies. Highlights differences from European patriarchal inheritance systems.
Land use Many Native peoples recognized community rights to use land, although practices varied widely; Europeans more often emphasized exclusive private ownership. Conflicting ideas about land contributed to later misunderstandings and warfare.

The Columbian Exchange and Spanish colonization

Questions of the day: What major changes did the Columbian Exchange cause on both sides of the Atlantic? How did European contact affect Indigenous nations?

MKC term or date What it means How it answers the question
1492 Christopher Columbus reached the Caribbean while sailing for Spain. His voyage began sustained contact among Europe, Africa, and the Americas. It marks the start of lasting biological exchange, European conquest, Atlantic migration, and severe disruption for Native societies.
Christopher Columbus A Genoese mariner sponsored by Ferdinand and Isabella who sought a westward route to Asia. He claimed Caribbean lands for Spain and helped initiate Spanish colonization. His voyages connected the hemispheres and opened the way for conquest, forced labor, Christian missions, and the Columbian Exchange.
Conquistadors Spanish conquerors such as Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro who sought wealth and territory. They used steel weapons, Native allies, political divisions, and the effects of disease to defeat large empires. Their conquests transferred political power and resources to Spain while devastating and reorganizing Indigenous societies.
Columbian Exchange The movement of plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492. American crops supported Old World population growth; European animals changed American life; disease caused catastrophic Native population loss; and plantation crops expanded coerced labor and slavery.
Spanish government in America Spain governed through royal officials, viceroyalties, audiencias, and local administrators under the authority of the crown and Council of the Indies. The church also played a major role through missions. Centralized government helped Spain control territory, extract wealth, regulate labor, and spread Catholicism.
Spanish social hierarchy in America A caste system placed peninsulares above criollos, followed by mixed-ancestry groups such as mestizos and mulattoes, with Native peoples and enslaved Africans generally at the bottom. Contact created new populations but assigned rights and power according to birthplace, ancestry, and race.
Haciendas Large Spanish-owned agricultural estates that produced food or goods for regional markets, often using dependent or coerced workers. They concentrated land and wealth in Spanish hands and reshaped Native labor, settlement, and local economies.
Bartolomé de las Casas A Spanish Dominican friar who condemned violence and exploitation of Native peoples and argued that they should be converted peacefully. His criticism helped inspire reforms and an Atlantic debate over the morality of conquest, although exploitation continued.
Encomienda and repartimiento systems Encomienda allowed colonists to demand Native tribute or labor in return for supposed protection and instruction. Repartimiento later required communities to provide workers for limited periods. Both systems supplied labor for the Spanish economy and disrupted Native communities despite attempted reforms.
Black Legend The image of Spain as uniquely cruel, intolerant, and exploitative, promoted by Spain's Protestant European rivals. It drew on real abuses but was also imperial propaganda. It shaped European competition by giving rival powers a moral argument for challenging Spain and creating their own colonies.

Nice to know

Term Short description How it applies
Smallpox An Old World disease to which Native Americans had little immunity. Epidemics caused enormous population loss and weakened resistance to conquest.
Horses Reintroduced to the Americas by Europeans after having disappeared there long before contact. Horses transformed transportation, hunting, trade, and warfare for some Native nations.
Mestizo A person of mixed European and Indigenous ancestry. Shows the demographic and cultural mixing created by colonization.
Pueblo Revolt connection In 1680, Pueblo peoples temporarily drove the Spanish from New Mexico after resisting forced labor and religious suppression. Although it occurred after Unit 1, it illustrates long-term Indigenous resistance to the Spanish system.

Comparing Spanish, French, and Dutch colonization

Question of the day: What were the similarities and differences among Spanish, French, and Dutch colonization?

MKC term or date What it means How it answers the question
French explorations Jacques Cartier explored the St. Lawrence River in the 1530s; Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec in 1608. French explorers sought trade routes, imperial claims, and commercial opportunities. France, like Spain and the Netherlands, sought wealth and territory, but French expansion centered more heavily on waterways, trade, and Native alliances.
Northwest Passage A hoped-for water route through North America to Asia. French, Dutch, and English explorers searched for it but instead mapped rivers and coastlines valuable for trade and settlement. The search encouraged exploration and competing claims even though the desired passage was not found.
French colonization / New France New France began at Quebec in 1608 and extended through the St. Lawrence Valley, Great Lakes, and later the Mississippi Valley. Its settler population remained small—about 70,000 by 1754—compared with British colonies. A small population and fur-based economy made cooperation with Native trading partners more important than acquiring extensive farmland.
French interaction with Indigenous peoples French traders and missionaries often lived near Native communities, learned local languages, and formed trade and military alliances. Conflict still occurred, and trade could create dependency or intensify Native rivalries. French survival and profit relied more on Native cooperation than did the land-hungry agricultural systems of many Spanish and British settlers. See 20260825 Reading.
Métis People of mixed French and Indigenous ancestry who formed distinct communities, especially around the fur trade. Many served as cultural and commercial intermediaries. Their communities show that French colonization produced cultural exchange and family alliances as well as imperial competition.
Henry Hudson An English explorer sailing for the Dutch who explored the Hudson River in 1609 while searching for a route to Asia. His reports encouraged Dutch fur trading in the region. His voyage helped establish the Dutch commercial claim to New Netherland. See 20260825 Reading.
Dutch West India Company A chartered joint-stock company established in 1621 to conduct Atlantic trade and manage Dutch colonies, including New Netherland. Dutch colonization was organized primarily around private commerce and profit rather than mass settlement or conversion.
New Netherland A Dutch colony founded in the 1620s along the Hudson River, with New Amsterdam on Manhattan as its main port. It connected Atlantic shipping to the inland fur trade. Its location and diverse population reflected Dutch commercial priorities; Britain seized it in 1664 and renamed it New York.
Dutch religious freedom New Netherland tolerated a wider range of religious communities than many contemporary colonies, although tolerance was incomplete and sometimes contested. Commercial leaders valued social stability and trade, helping produce a more diverse colony than tightly controlled Spanish or Puritan settlements.
Women's rights in New Netherland Dutch law allowed married women more control over property, inheritance, contracts, and business than English common law generally did. Some women acted as merchants or went to court. Dutch legal culture produced different gender opportunities from those in many English and Spanish colonies.
Settlement of New Netherland The colony attracted Dutch, German, Scandinavian, Jewish, African, and other residents, but its European population remained small—about 9,000 by the mid-1660s. The company offered land and livestock to attract settlers. Its small, diverse, trade-focused population contrasts with Spain's conquest system and the larger agricultural settlements of British North America. See 20260825 Reading.
Patroons Wealthy investors who received large estates for bringing settlers to New Netherland. Tenant farmers worked their land under the patroonship system. The policy was meant to increase settlement while preserving company profit and creating a powerful landholding elite.
New Netherland interaction with Indigenous peoples Dutch traders depended on Native suppliers for furs and formed commercial alliances, especially with the Haudenosaunee. Land disputes and Dutch expansion also produced violence. As with the French, trade encouraged cooperation, but competition for resources and settlement could turn relationships into conflict.
Borderlands in early America Contested regions where European empires and Native nations met without any one group exercising complete control. Trade, warfare, diplomacy, migration, and cultural mixing shaped these zones. Borderlands reveal that colonization was negotiated and resisted; Native nations remained active powers rather than passive victims of European expansion.

Nice to know

Term Short description How it applies
Fur trade Native suppliers exchanged pelts for European manufactured goods. It was the economic foundation of both New France and New Netherland and encouraged alliances.
Joint-stock company Investors pooled money and shared the risks and profits of overseas ventures. It allowed Dutch and later English colonization to be financed through private enterprise.
New Amsterdam The diverse Dutch port at the southern end of Manhattan. It connected the Hudson River fur trade to the Atlantic economy.
Haudenosaunee diplomacy The confederacy used alliances and trade to protect its power amid competition among European empires. Native nations influenced imperial rivalry and pursued their own political goals.

One-minute comparison

  • Spanish: conquest, mining, missions, large territorial claims, and coerced Native labor.
  • French: fur trade, few settlers, waterways, missionaries, and extensive Native alliances.
  • Dutch: commerce, joint-stock companies, religious diversity, a small population, and the Hudson River fur trade.
  • Shared pattern: all sought wealth and imperial power, relied on Native knowledge or trade, introduced major cultural and ecological changes, and eventually contributed to conflict over land and resources.
