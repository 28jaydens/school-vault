AP US History — Periods 1 and 2 Test Prep

← Exams & Reviews · Concept Map · Class Home

Period 1: 1491–1607

AP Exam weighting: 4–6%

Topic 1.1 — Contextualizing Period 1

Learning objective: Explain the context for European encounters in the Americas from 1491–1607.

Historical developments

  • Before European contact, millions of Native Americans lived in diverse societies throughout the Americas.
  • Native societies developed distinct political, economic, and cultural systems in response to different environments.
  • European states sought wealth, trade routes, territory, and converts as maritime technology improved and competition among empires intensified.
  • Contact after 1492 joined the Americas, Europe, and Africa in a new Atlantic system of migration, trade, conquest, disease, and cultural exchange.
  • The resulting demographic collapse of Native populations and growing European demand for labor transformed the Atlantic world.

Topic 1.2 — Native American Societies Before European Contact

Thematic focus: Geography and the Environment (GEO)

Learning objective: Explain how and why Native populations interacted with the natural environment before European contact.

Historical developments

  • In the arid Southwest, Pueblo peoples used irrigation and lived in permanent adobe settlements; scarcity of water shaped agriculture and community organization.
  • In the Great Basin and Great Plains, many groups relied on hunting and gathering and moved seasonally to follow available resources.
  • In the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys, fertile land supported maize agriculture, large settlements, and complex societies such as Cahokia.
  • In the Northeast, the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) combined agriculture with hunting and organized a powerful political confederacy.
  • In the Atlantic Seaboard, groups such as the Powhatan lived in settled agricultural communities and controlled regional trade networks.
  • Native peoples altered their environments through irrigation, farming, hunting, and controlled burning rather than living separately from nature.

Topic 1.3 — European Exploration in the Americas

Thematic focus: America in the World (WOR)

Learning objective: Explain the causes of exploration and conquest by European nations.

Historical developments

  • European rulers pursued the “three Gs”: gold, glory, and God.
  • The desire for direct access to Asian goods encouraged Europeans to search for western sea routes.
  • Competition among Spain, Portugal, France, England, and the Netherlands drove claims to land and resources.
  • New tools and knowledge—including caravels, compasses, astrolabes, and improved maps—made long-distance voyages more practical.
  • Spain used conquistadores and missionaries to establish a territorial empire; France and the Netherlands initially emphasized commerce, especially the fur trade.
  • Joint-stock companies later allowed private investors to share the costs and risks of colonization. This model supported Dutch and English expansion. See 20260825 Reading.

Topic 1.4 — Columbian Exchange, Spanish Exploration, and Conquest

Thematic focus: Geography and the Environment (GEO)

Learning objective: Explain the causes and effects of the Columbian Exchange after 1492.

Historical developments

  • Sustained contact after Columbus's voyages transferred plants, animals, diseases, people, and ideas between hemispheres.
  • Europeans introduced horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, and sugar to the Americas; American crops such as maize, potatoes, and tomatoes spread to Europe and beyond.
  • European diseases—especially smallpox—killed a very large share of Native populations because they lacked immunity.
  • Horses transformed travel, hunting, and warfare for some Native societies, particularly on the Great Plains
  • American food crops contributed to population growth in Europe, while sugar cultivation helped expand plantation labor and Atlantic slavery.
  • Spanish conquistadores defeated the Aztec and Inca empires through military technology, Native alliances, internal divisions, and epidemic disease.
  • Spain extracted wealth through mining and agriculture and attempted to convert Native peoples to Christianity.

Topic 1.5 — Labor, Slavery, and Caste in the Spanish Colonial System

Thematic focus: Social Structures (SOC)

Learning objective: Explain how Spanish imperial growth shaped social and economic structures.

Historical developments

  • The encomienda system granted Spanish colonists the right to demand labor or tribute from Native communities in exchange for supposed protection and Christian instruction.
  • Abuse, warfare, and disease devastated Native communities, leading Spain to reform—but not end—coerced labor.
  • The repartimiento system required Native communities to supply workers for limited periods.
  • As the Native labor force declined, Europeans increasingly imported enslaved Africans.
  • Spanish colonies developed a caste hierarchy: peninsulares held the highest status, followed by criollos, mixed-race groups such as mestizos and mulattoes, Native peoples, and enslaved Africans.
  • Spanish law sometimes recognized limited rights involving marriage, property, and manumission, although enslaved people remained subject to coercion and exploitation. Compare 20260827 Reading.

Topic 1.6 — Cultural Interactions Between Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans

Thematic focus: America in the World (WOR)

Learning objective: Explain how and why European and Native perspectives of one another developed and changed.

Historical developments

  • Europeans often judged Native societies according to Christian beliefs, private-property traditions, and European ideas of “civilization.”
  • Native peoples initially viewed Europeans as potential trade or military partners but responded differently as colonization threatened land, sovereignty, and culture.
  • Europeans tried to convert Native peoples, while Native communities adapted, resisted, or blended Christianity with existing beliefs.
  • The Spanish debated the treatment of Native peoples: Bartolomé de las Casas condemned colonial brutality, while Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda defended conquest and forced labor.
  • Trade created cultural exchange and military alliances but also increased Native dependence on European goods and intensified competition among Native groups.
  • Africans carried languages, beliefs, agricultural knowledge, and cultural practices into the Americas despite enslavement.

Period 2: 1607–1754

AP Exam weighting: 6–8%

Topic 2.1 — Contextualizing Period 2

Learning objective: Explain the context for colonization from 1607–1754.

Historical developments

  • After 1607, Spain, France, the Netherlands, and England built colonies with different economic goals, settlement patterns, and relationships with Native peoples.
  • England's first permanent settlement at Jamestown began as a profit-seeking Virginia Company venture. See 20260818 Reading.
  • Colonies became connected to an Atlantic economy built on trade, migration, indentured labor, and increasingly racial slavery.
  • Environmental conditions and available resources produced distinct colonial regions.
  • Colonial expansion repeatedly created conflict over Native land while trade also encouraged alliances and cultural exchange.

Topic 2.2 — European Colonization

Thematic focus: Geography and the Environment (GEO)

Learning objective: Explain how and why European colonies developed and expanded.

Historical developments

  • Spanish colonies emphasized conquest, missions, mining, and coerced labor.
  • French colonies had relatively small European populations and depended on fur-trading alliances with Native peoples. See 20260825 Reading.
  • Dutch settlement centered on commerce and the fur trade; the Dutch West India Company established New Netherland and a settlement on Manhattan. See 20260825 Reading.
  • English colonies attracted larger settler populations and developed agriculture, representative institutions, and permanent communities.
  • Jamestown struggled with hunger, disease, poor leadership, and settlers' focus on gold before tobacco made Virginia profitable. See 20260818 Reading.
  • Puritans migrated largely as families to build religious communities in New England, producing more stable population growth. See 20260821 Reading.

Topic 2.3 — The Regions of British Colonies

Thematic focus: Geography and the Environment (GEO)

Learning objective: Explain how environmental and other factors shaped British colonial regions.

Historical developments

  • New England's rocky soil and long winters encouraged small family farms, fishing, logging, shipbuilding, and commerce rather than plantation agriculture.
  • The fertile Middle Colonies exported wheat and corn and developed diverse populations and trading cities such as New York and Philadelphia.
  • The Chesapeake and Lower South relied on cash crops: tobacco in Virginia and Maryland, and rice and indigo farther south.
  • Southern plantations' labor demands encouraged indentured servitude and then racial slavery.
  • New England settlements often consisted of Puritan families, whereas early Chesapeake settlement included many young single men seeking profit.
  • These environmental and demographic differences created lasting regional variation. See 20260931 Reading and 20260818 Reading.

Topic 2.4 — Transatlantic Trade

Thematic focus: Work, Exchange, and Technology (WXT)

Learning objective: Explain causes and effects of transatlantic trade.

Historical developments

  • European demand for tobacco, sugar, rice, indigo, and furs connected the colonies to overseas markets.
  • French and Dutch fur traders exchanged European manufactured goods for furs supplied by Native partners. See 20260825 Reading.
  • Tobacco transformed Virginia into a profitable colony while increasing demand for land and labor. See 20260818 Reading.
  • Joint-stock companies pooled investors' money and spread the financial risk of colonial ventures.
  • Atlantic commerce brought investment and goods to the colonies but also expanded the forced migration of enslaved Africans.
  • Sugar's profitability drove the growth of brutal plantation slavery in Brazil and the Caribbean, providing a model for English colonies. See 20260827 Reading.

Topic 2.5 — Interactions Between American Indians and Europeans

Thematic focus: America in the World (WOR)

Learning objective: Explain why interactions between Europeans and Native Americans changed over time.

Historical developments

  • Europeans often depended on Native peoples for food, geographic knowledge, trade, and military alliances.
  • The French generally built trading alliances because the survival of New France depended on Native suppliers of furs. See 20260825 Reading.
  • At Jamestown, the English initially depended on the Powhatan for food, and Pocahontas's marriage to John Rolfe helped produce a period of peace.
  • English demand for tobacco land increased conflict; Opechancanough's 1622 uprising killed about one-quarter of Virginia's settlers but did not halt expansion. See 20260818 Reading.
  • Trade gave Native peoples access to useful European goods but could create dependency and alter hunting patterns.
  • As permanent European settlement expanded, relationships often shifted from trade and cooperation toward warfare and Native displacement.

Topic 2.6 — Slavery in the British Colonies

Thematic focus: Work, Exchange, and Technology (WXT); Social Structures (SOC)

Learning objectives: Explain the causes and effects of slavery and how enslaved people responded.

Historical developments

  • Labor-intensive plantation crops created sustained demand for workers.
  • Chesapeake planters initially preferred cheaper indentured servants, but declining immigration and expanding tobacco production encouraged permanent African slavery.
  • The first twenty Africans arrived in Virginia in 1619; by the 1660s, Virginia and Maryland explicitly recognized slavery in law.
  • A 1662 Virginia law made a child's status follow that of the mother, helping make slavery hereditary.
  • Slavery became increasingly racialized, producing a sharp legal and social division between Black slavery and white freedom.
  • Caribbean sugar plantations demonstrated the enormous profits possible through large-scale enslaved labor.
  • Enslaved people resisted by preserving family and culture, slowing work, breaking tools, escaping, and sometimes rebelling.
  • See 20260827 Reading for the development of racial slavery and 20260931 Reading for its regional economic role.

Topic 2.7 — Colonial Society and Culture

Thematic focus: American and Regional Culture (ARC); American and National Identity (NAT)

Learning objectives: Explain how Atlantic movement shaped culture and how colonists' goals affected their relationship with Britain.

Historical developments

  • Immigration and natural increase expanded the colonial population from about 250,000 in 1701 to 2.5 million in 1775.
  • English, German, Scotch-Irish, French, African, and other peoples made colonial society increasingly diverse.
  • Puritans defined “moral liberty” as the freedom to do what was morally right within a disciplined religious community. See 20260821 Reading.
  • The Mayflower Compact established government by majority agreement, while Virginia's House of Burgesses became the first elected colonial assembly; political participation remained limited.
  • Religious patterns differed by region: New England had strong Congregationalist influence, the Middle Colonies were more diverse, and Anglicanism dominated much of the South.
  • The Great Awakening encouraged personal religious judgment, weakened some established churches, expanded denominations, and became a shared intercolonial experience.
  • Salutary neglect and experience with local self-government encouraged colonists to develop interests and identities distinct from British officials.
  • See 20260931 Reading, 20260821 Reading, and 20260818 Reading.

High-value comparisons

Comparison Key distinction
Spanish vs. French colonization Spanish conquest, missions, and coerced labor vs. French trade alliances and small settler populations
French/Dutch vs. English relations with Native peoples Trade-centered partnerships vs. larger English settlements that demanded increasing amounts of land
New England vs. Chesapeake Family migration, small farms, and religious communities vs. cash crops, young migrants, and labor demand
Indentured servitude vs. slavery Time-limited contractual labor vs. permanent, hereditary, and increasingly race-based bondage
Period 1 vs. Period 2 Initial contact and exchange vs. permanent colonies, regional economies, and expanding Atlantic systems

  • 20260821 Reading — Puritanism, Plymouth, migration, family, and moral liberty
  • 20260825 Reading — French and Dutch trade, Native alliances, and Atlantic commerce
  • 20260827 Reading — plantation labor and the development of racial slavery
  • 20260931 Reading — population, immigration, regional economies, religion, and the Great Awakening
