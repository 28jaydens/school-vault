AP US History — Unit 2 MKC (1607–1754)

← Exams & Reviews · Study Guide · Concept Map

Completed MKC worksheet

Definitions explain each term in plain language. Significance connects it to a larger historical development. Reading-page references are preserved from the worksheet; the textbook itself was not provided.

Key concepts for the unit

Key Concept 1.2 — Contact and the Columbian Exchange

This is background from the earlier period that helps explain Unit 2.

  • European competition: European states sought wealth, trade routes, converts, and imperial power. Rivalries encouraged exploration, colonization, and conflict in the Americas.
  • Columbian Exchange and the Spanish Empire: Crops, animals, people, and diseases moved across the Atlantic. Introduced diseases devastated Indigenous populations, while European conquest and plantation production expanded coerced labor and African slavery.
  • Different worldviews: Europeans and Indigenous peoples often understood religion, gender, landholding, and political authority differently. Trade and diplomacy could bridge differences, but colonial expansion repeatedly produced conflict. Native societies were diverse and responded in different ways.

Key Concept 2.1 — Different patterns of colonization

  • Different imperial goals: Spain emphasized conquest, missions, and extracting wealth; France and the Netherlands emphasized trade and Native alliances; England established extensive farming settlements. These patterns varied within each empire.
  • Regional British differences: New England developed towns, family farms, fishing, and commerce; the Middle Colonies developed grain farming, trade, and religious diversity; the Chesapeake and Lower South increasingly relied on plantation crops and enslaved labor.
  • Competition and conflict: Colonists competed with European rivals and Indigenous nations over land, trade, and resources. Native nations used diplomacy, alliances, trade, and warfare to defend their interests.

Key Concept 2.2 — Atlantic connections and colonial change

  • Ties to Britain and resistance: Trade, migration, Protestant religion, and British political ideas connected the colonies to Britain. Colonial assemblies and uneven enforcement of trade rules also encouraged habits of local government. Enlightenment ideas and the Great Awakening challenged established authority in different ways.
  • Slavery: Colonies created laws that made slavery increasingly racial and hereditary. Plantation agriculture expanded the demand for enslaved Africans, while regional economies produced different work systems. Enslaved people maintained communities and resisted exploitation.

1. Chesapeake Bay Colonies

Relevant readings: pages 54–61.

Question of the Day: To what extent did indentured servants and enslaved people play a role in the development of colonies in America?

Answer: They played a central role, especially in plantation colonies. Indentured servants supplied much of the Chesapeake's early tobacco labor, while enslaved Africans became increasingly important during the late 1600s and early 1700s. Their labor generated exports and wealth for planters, encouraged plantation expansion, and shaped sharp class and racial inequalities. Geography, trade, and colonial government also influenced development, and family labor remained important in other regions.

MKC term What it is, in plain language Why it is significant
Early British exploration English voyages such as John Cabot's 1497 expedition and the failed Roanoke settlements of the 1580s preceded permanent colonization. Established English claims and showed the risks of settlement. Trade, rivalry with Spain, and hopes for profit encouraged later colonies.
Mercantilism The belief that colonies should strengthen the home country's wealth and power by supplying raw materials and buying its goods. Explains imperial trade restrictions and why Britain valued colonial exports such as tobacco.
Indentured servants People bound by contracts to work for a set period, often four to seven years, in exchange for passage and support. Supplied much of the early Chesapeake labor force. Harsh treatment and limited opportunities after service contributed to social tension. Source
Reaction of Indigenous nations to English colonists Native nations traded, negotiated, formed alliances, or fought, depending on local interests and circumstances. Shows Native agency. English land expansion increasingly threatened Indigenous sovereignty and made peaceful relations harder to maintain.
Joint-stock company A business in which investors pooled money and shared financial risk, such as the Virginia Company. Financed expensive overseas settlements and made the search for profit a major force in colonization.
Jamestown A settlement founded in Virginia in 1607 by the Virginia Company; the first permanent English settlement in what became the United States. Established England's lasting foothold in the Chesapeake and developed into a tobacco-centered colony.
John Smith An early Jamestown leader who imposed work rules and negotiated, traded, and sometimes used force in dealings with local Native peoples. Helped the settlement survive its early years; his actions show both colonial dependence on Native resources and growing conflict.
Powhatan Nation The alliance of Algonquian-speaking peoples in the Virginia region led by Wahunsenacawh, often called Powhatan. Was a major regional power. Its relations with Jamestown shaped the survival of the English colony and the struggle over Native land.
Pocahontas Powhatan's daughter, also known as Amonute and Matoaka; captured by the English in 1613 and married to John Rolfe in 1614. Her marriage helped establish a temporary peace. Her story also shows how colonial diplomacy involved coercion and how English promoters used Native people to advertise colonization.
Tobacco A labor-intensive cash crop grown for sale, especially to European consumers. Made Chesapeake settlement profitable and increased demand for land, indentured labor, and later enslaved labor.
John Rolfe A Virginia colonist who developed a tobacco variety marketable in England beginning around 1612; he married Pocahontas. Helped establish the tobacco economy that shaped Virginia's settlement and labor systems.
Headright system A Virginia land incentive that generally awarded 50 acres for paying a new immigrant's passage. Encouraged migration and helped wealthy colonists accumulate land by financing the passage of laborers. Source
House of Burgesses Virginia's elected representatives first met in the General Assembly in 1619; they became a separate legislative chamber in 1643. Established an important tradition of representative colonial government, although political participation was limited and elites held substantial power.
Powhatan Wars Three major wars between Virginia colonists and Powhatan peoples: 1609–1614, 1622–1632, and 1644–1646. Expanded English territorial control and severely reduced Powhatan political independence, without ending the existence of Native communities.
Uprising of 1622 A coordinated attack on Virginia settlements organized under Opechancanough to resist English expansion. Killed roughly a quarter of the colony's English population and led to prolonged English retaliation and further land seizure.
Opechancanough A Powhatan leader associated with the major anti-English attacks of 1622 and 1644. Represents organized Indigenous resistance to settlement and efforts to preserve Native independence.
Maryland A proprietary colony founded in 1634 under the Calvert family, intended partly as a refuge for English Catholics. Combined a tobacco economy with conflict over religion and political power in a colony where Protestants became the majority.
Act of Toleration, 1649 A Maryland law protecting worship for Christians who accepted the Trinity, including Catholics. Was an early but limited example of religious toleration; it did not establish freedom of religion for everyone.
Dower rights A widow's legal claim to use part of her deceased husband's property during her lifetime, commonly one-third of his land. Offered some economic protection to widows while leaving the broader male-centered property system in place. It was a life interest, not necessarily full ownership. Historical example

Nice to Know

Term Short description How it applies to the question
Cash crop A crop grown mainly to sell rather than to feed the grower's household. Tobacco profits help explain planters' demand for large labor forces.
Freedom dues Money, clothing, tools, or other supplies sometimes owed to servants when their contracts ended. Servants could eventually gain freedom, although resources and opportunities afterward were often limited.
Chattel slavery A system treating enslaved people as legally owned property who could be bought and sold. Explains the fundamental difference between a temporary labor contract and permanent enslavement.
Coverture English legal rules placing a married woman's legal and property rights largely under her husband's authority. Shows how labor and wealth were organized through both gender and status. Widows could exercise rights unavailable to many married women. Source

2. New England and the Rest of the Colonies

Relevant readings: pages 64–71.

Question of the Day: To what extent did religion play a role in the development of the New England colonies?

Answer: Religion strongly shaped New England's founding, government, education, and community life. Pilgrims established Plymouth as Separatists, while Puritans created Massachusetts Bay as a model Christian society. Religious expectations supported schooling and strict moral rules, but dissenters such as Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson faced exclusion. Religion was therefore central, although geography, family migration, commerce, and relationships with Native peoples also shaped the region.

MKC term What it is, in plain language Why it is significant
Puritans English Protestants who wanted to reform the Church of England by removing practices they considered too Catholic. Founded Massachusetts Bay and shaped New England's religious institutions, education, and moral expectations.
John Winthrop A leading Puritan and longtime Massachusetts Bay governor who envisioned the colony as an example of a godly community. Connected religious purpose with colonial government and community discipline.
Pilgrims English Separatists who believed they should leave the Church of England and established Plymouth in 1620. Demonstrate how religious dissent motivated migration; unlike most Puritans, they sought separation rather than reform from within.
Mayflower The ship that brought the Pilgrims and other settlers to New England in 1620. Its landing outside the intended Virginia jurisdiction prompted the settlers to create their own governing agreement.
Mayflower Compact A 1620 agreement by the colony's adult male signers to establish government and obey laws made for the community. An early example of government based on an agreement among settlers; it did not create universal voting rights.
Plymouth Colony The New England colony founded by the Pilgrims in 1620; later incorporated into Massachusetts in 1691. Became an early model of a religious settler community whose survival depended partly on Native diplomacy and knowledge.
Massasoit Ousamequin, a Wampanoag leader who formed an alliance with Plymouth in 1621. Sought advantages for his people amid regional rivalries and disease; the alliance helped Plymouth survive.
Squanto Tisquantum, a Patuxet man who had been kidnapped and taken overseas before returning; he interpreted and advised Plymouth settlers. His language skills and agricultural knowledge helped the colonists and show the importance of Native intermediaries.
Samoset An Abenaki leader who greeted Plymouth settlers in 1621 and helped connect them with other Native leaders. Helped open communication between Plymouth and the region's Native peoples.
The Great Migration The movement of thousands of English Puritans to New England during the 1630s, often in family groups. Built populous religious communities and contributed to New England's relatively stable family structure.
Massachusetts Bay Company The chartered company through which Puritan leaders organized Massachusetts Bay settlement, beginning in 1630. Moving the charter and leadership to New England allowed considerable local control and helped create a religiously organized colony.
Boston A town founded in 1630 that became Massachusetts Bay's main political and commercial center. Shows the combination of Puritan community life with an expanding Atlantic trading economy.
Roger Williams A minister banished from Massachusetts who founded Providence in 1636 and defended freedom of conscience and separation of church and state. Helped establish Rhode Island as a refuge for dissenters and challenged colonial claims to Native land without purchase.
Anne Hutchinson A religious dissenter who criticized Puritan ministers and emphasized salvation through God's grace; Massachusetts banished her in 1637. Revealed limits on dissent and women's public religious authority in Puritan society.
Pequot War A 1636–1637 war involving the Pequot, English colonists, and Native allies; the attack at Mystic killed hundreds of Pequots. Weakened Pequot power and opened more land to English settlement, illustrating the violence of colonial expansion.
New York A colony taken by England from the Dutch in 1664; previously part of New Netherland. Retained ethnic and religious diversity and became an important commercial center linking the northern and southern English colonies.
Pennsylvania A colony founded by William Penn in 1681 with Quaker influence, religious toleration, and an elected assembly. Attracted diverse settlers and became a major center of grain farming and trade.
William Penn The Quaker proprietor of Pennsylvania who promoted religious toleration and negotiated land purchases with Native peoples. His policies offered an alternative to religious uniformity, although later colonial expansion undermined peaceful Native relations.
Carolinas Colonies originating in a 1663 proprietary grant; the northern area developed smaller farms, while the southern lowcountry developed rice plantations. Demonstrate how geography and Caribbean migration produced different regional economies and especially extensive slavery in South Carolina.
Georgia A colony chartered in 1732 and settled in 1733 as a buffer against Spanish Florida and an experiment in settlement for the poor. Its early restrictions on slavery and landholding faced planter opposition; slavery became legal in 1751.
13 Colonies New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Georgia. Their different economies, populations, and religious traditions complicate the idea of one uniform colonial society.
Navigation Acts English laws beginning in 1651 that restricted colonial shipping and directed important parts of colonial trade through England and its empire. Put mercantilism into practice, strengthened Atlantic connections, and encouraged smuggling and resentment of imperial controls.

Nice to Know

Term Short description How it applies to the question
Congregational church A church system in which each local congregation governed many of its own affairs. Connected Puritan religion with locally organized community life.
Town meetings Local assemblies in which eligible residents, generally qualifying men, made community decisions. Show how New England towns developed local participation alongside religious and social restrictions.
Half-Way Covenant, 1662 Allowed baptism for children of baptized church members who had not described a conversion experience. Shows Puritan churches adjusting to declining full membership across generations.
Religious dissent Disagreement with established religious beliefs or church authority. Williams and Hutchinson show that migration for religious reasons did not automatically produce toleration.

3. Slavery and Resistance — Development of Colonial Systems

Relevant readings: pages 88–94.

Question of the Day, as printed: To what extent did religion play a role in the development of the British colonies?

Answer: Religion had a major but uneven role. Puritan beliefs shaped Massachusetts, Quaker ideals influenced Pennsylvania, and Catholic–Protestant conflict shaped Maryland. Religion also supported hierarchy: colonists sometimes used Christianity to justify domination, while enslaved people preserved or adapted religious practices as sources of community. Economic goals were especially powerful in plantation colonies. Virginia's 1667 decision that baptism did not end enslavement shows lawmakers protecting slaveholders' interests despite religious conversion.

The printed question repeats a religion theme even though this section mainly covers slavery and resistance. The Pueblo Revolt concerns Spanish, rather than British, colonization.

MKC term What it is, in plain language Why it is significant
Development of slavery in the “New World” European colonizers expanded the enslavement of Indigenous people and Africans to supply labor for plantations, mines, and households. Helped build Atlantic economies and racial hierarchies through forced labor and displacement.
Pueblo Revolt, 1680 An uprising led by Po'pay and other Pueblo leaders against Spanish religious repression and colonial demands in New Mexico. Drove Spanish colonists out for about 12 years and demonstrates successful coordinated Indigenous resistance. Source
Development of slavery in English colonies A gradual process through which colonial laws and practices made African enslavement increasingly permanent, hereditary, and racial. Slavery was constructed over time rather than appearing as one complete legal system in 1619.
Plantation A large agricultural estate producing cash crops for sale, usually through a controlled labor force. Linked landownership, export markets, and coerced labor; wealthy plantation owners gained major economic and political influence.
Slavery in the West Indies The extensive use of enslaved African labor on Caribbean plantations, especially for sugar production. Created enormous profits alongside extreme mortality and helped provide a model for mainland plantation slavery.
Barbados An English Caribbean colony that became a major sugar producer using enslaved Africans; it adopted a major slave code in 1661. Migrants carried plantation practices and laws from Barbados to Carolina, connecting Caribbean and mainland slavery.
Las Siete Partidas A medieval Spanish legal code that regulated slavery while recognizing limited claims concerning marriage and freedom under certain conditions. Gave some enslaved people legal routes to claim rights or freedom, although slavery remained coercive and protections were unevenly enforced. Historical overview
Spanish slavery vs. English slavery Spanish law generally recognized more routes to manumission and some family rights; English colonial codes increasingly emphasized permanent racial ownership. Shows differences between imperial legal systems. Both systems exploited enslaved people; legal provisions do not prove that daily treatment was humane.
The rise of Chesapeake slavery Planters increasingly relied on enslaved Africans in the late 1600s as access to the slave trade expanded and permanent labor became more profitable. Transformed tobacco production and helped consolidate a powerful planter class and a hereditary racial hierarchy. Source
John Punch An African servant in Virginia sentenced to lifetime servitude in 1640 after escaping with two European servants, who received extended terms instead. An early documented example of racial differences being imposed through colonial punishment and labor law.
Bacon's Rebellion A 1676–1677 uprising led by Nathaniel Bacon against Virginia governor William Berkeley; rebels also attacked Native peoples, including English allies. Exposed conflicts over land and elite rule and fears of unrest among laborers. It occurred during a gradual expansion of slavery; it did not single-handedly create that system. Source
Metacom / King Philip's War A 1675–1676 war in southern New England between Native forces associated with Metacom and English colonists with Native allies; fighting continued farther north. Devastated communities, reduced Native power in southern New England, and led to the enslavement of many Native captives.
Slave codes Colonial laws defining enslaved people as property and restricting their movement, assembly, and other rights. Made racial inequality enforceable and protected slaveholders' power through law and punishment.
The spread of slavery The expansion of enslaved labor across plantation regions and into northern farms, ports, trades, and households. Shows that slavery was tied to the economy of the entire colonial Atlantic world, even where large plantations were uncommon.

Nice to Know

Term Short description How it applies to the question
Partus sequitur ventrem The rule, adopted in Virginia in 1662, that a child's free or enslaved status followed the mother's. Made slavery hereditary and protected slaveholders' claims even when a child's father was free.
Virginia baptism law, 1667 Declared that Christian baptism did not change an enslaved person's legal status. Direct evidence that religious conversion could coexist with permanent enslavement.
Manumission The legal freeing of an enslaved person by an enslaver. Helps compare imperial systems and the limited paths to legal freedom.
Established church A church officially supported by a colonial government, often through taxes. Shows how religion and political authority were connected, including Anglican establishments in the Chesapeake.

4. Slavery and Resistance — Communities and Freedom

Relevant readings: pages 99–102 and 138–139.

Question of the Day: To what extent did marginalized communities attempt to gain or regain power and freedom in 17th-century America?

Answer: Marginalized communities repeatedly sought freedom through armed resistance, escape, negotiation, legal claims, and cultural survival. In the 1600s, the Pueblo Revolt temporarily restored Indigenous independence, while Metacom's resistance ended in major Native losses. Enslaved people also resisted through flight and daily efforts to preserve family and community. These actions challenged colonial power, but military defeat, punishment, and restrictive laws often limited lasting gains.

Chronology note: The New York uprising of 1712 and Stono Rebellion of 1739 are 18th-century examples. They fit Unit 2, but not a response restricted to the 17th century.

MKC term What it is, in plain language Why it is significant
The Atlantic slave trade The forced transportation and sale of millions of Africans across the Atlantic, mostly to Brazil and the Caribbean. Supplied labor for colonial economies, enriched merchants and enslavers, and created the African diaspora.
Middle Passage The ocean crossing during which captured Africans were transported to the Americas in overcrowded, violent, and deadly conditions. Central to the brutality of the slave trade; captives resisted even during the voyage.
Asiento A Spanish government contract granting the right to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish colonies; Britain received an important contract in 1713. Connected slavery with mercantilist monopolies, diplomacy, and competition among European empires. Source
Slavery's impact on Chesapeake colonies Enslaved labor became central to tobacco production, while laws and wealth strengthened a slaveholding planter elite. Made race and inherited status major divisions in society and tied prosperity to forced labor.
Enslavement of Indigenous peoples Colonists and their partners captured and traded Native people through warfare and slave raiding, sometimes sending captives to distant colonies. Disrupted Native communities and linked territorial conquest to Atlantic labor markets. Virginia example
Rice Kingdom The rice-producing lowcountry of South Carolina and, later, coastal Georgia, where plantations depended heavily on enslaved Africans. African agricultural knowledge was important to production; large enslaved populations also sustained distinctive communities and traditions.
James Oglethorpe A founder and leading trustee of Georgia who supported settlement for the poor and initially opposed slavery in the colony. His plans show how a colonial social experiment could be undermined by demands for plantation profits.
Slavery in the North Enslaved people worked in northern households, workshops, ports, and farms; northern merchants also profited from slave trading and plantation commerce. Demonstrates that northern colonies participated directly in slavery, even though their economies differed from southern plantation societies.
African American culture New cultural traditions formed as Africans and their descendants preserved and combined languages, foodways, music, family practices, and beliefs. Helped people build community and identity despite forced displacement and attempts to control their lives.
African religion Diverse spiritual traditions carried by Africans, including beliefs involving ancestors, spiritual forces, and communal rituals; some Africans were Muslim or Christian. Religious practices helped sustain identity and resistance. Some traditions blended with Christianity, producing new forms of worship.
The three systems of enslavement In this regional context: Chesapeake tobacco plantations, Lowcountry rice plantations, and northern mixed farming and urban slavery. Shows that work, supervision, community life, and opportunities for resistance differed by region. Tobacco often used gang labor; rice often used task labor.
The Gullah community African-descended communities of the southeastern coastal lowcountry and Sea Islands, with a distinctive creole language and cultural traditions. Large African populations and coastal conditions helped people preserve and adapt cultural practices under slavery. Source
Resistance to slavery Actions including escape, work slowdowns, breaking tools, preserving family ties, legal petitions, and organized revolts. Shows that enslaved people actively pursued dignity, autonomy, and freedom rather than accepting enslavement.
Stono Rebellion, 1739 An uprising of enslaved people in South Carolina; participants moved south toward Spanish Florida, where some fugitives could obtain freedom. Demonstrates organized resistance and the use of imperial rivalries. Suppression was followed by South Carolina's stricter 1740 slave law.
New York Slave Uprising, 1712 An armed revolt in New York City in which enslaved participants set a fire and attacked whites who responded. Shows that resistance occurred in northern cities too; authorities responded with executions and tighter restrictions.

“The three systems of enslavement” is interpreted here as the three regional systems of mainland British North America, based on the surrounding terms. The textbook wording was not supplied.

Nice to Know

Term Short description How it applies to the question
Task system Workers were assigned a defined daily amount of labor, especially on rice plantations. Finishing tasks could leave some time for gardens, family, or cultural life, although the work remained coerced and demanding. Source
Gang system Groups worked together under close supervision for long periods, common in tobacco and sugar production. Explains differences in control over daily work and opportunities for autonomy.
Maroon communities Communities formed by people who escaped slavery, often in areas difficult for colonial authorities to reach. Demonstrate efforts to build lasting independence beyond an enslaver's control.
Cultural blending Combining traditions from different backgrounds, including African and Christian beliefs. Shows how people adapted to displacement while maintaining community and identity.

Common mistakes to avoid: The Mayflower Compact did not create universal democracy. Maryland's toleration law did not protect all religions.
