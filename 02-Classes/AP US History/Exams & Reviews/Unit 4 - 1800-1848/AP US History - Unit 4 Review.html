AP US History — Unit 4 Review (1800–1848)

← Unit 4 Hub · All Reviews

[!summary] Unit 4 in one sentence
From 1800 to 1848, the United States expanded westward, developed a national market economy, broadened political participation for white men, and experienced major religious and reform movements—while conflicts over federal power, Native land, race, gender, and slavery intensified.

1. The Election of 1800 and Jeffersonian Democracy

Election of 1800

  • Federalists: John Adams; favored a stronger national government, commerce, and closer relations with Britain.
  • Democratic-Republicans: Thomas Jefferson; favored limited federal power, an agrarian republic, states' rights, and sympathy toward France.
  • Jefferson and his running mate, Aaron Burr, each received 73 electoral votes because electors did not cast separate votes for president and vice president.
  • The House of Representatives selected Jefferson on the 36th ballot after Alexander Hamilton urged Federalists to oppose Burr.
  • The Twelfth Amendment (1804) required electors to cast separate votes for president and vice president.

“Revolution of 1800”

  • Power transferred peacefully from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans.
  • This proved that the constitutional system could survive a change in ruling parties.
  • Jefferson tried to reduce partisan tension: “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists.”

Aaron Burr

  • Burr killed Alexander Hamilton in an 1804 duel.
  • He later became involved in a plan to create an independent territory in the Southwest and possibly Mexico.
  • Burr was tried for treason in 1807 but acquitted because the Constitution requires proof of an overt act of treason.

2. The Marshall Court and Federal Power

Midnight judges

  • Before leaving office, Adams and the Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801 and appointed Federalist judges.
  • John Marshall, Adams's secretary of state, became chief justice and served from 1801 to 1835.
  • James Madison was Jefferson's secretary of state, not Adams's.

Marbury v. Madison (1803)

  • William Marbury's judicial commission had been signed but not delivered.
  • Marbury asked the Supreme Court to issue a writ of mandamus ordering Madison to deliver it.
  • Marshall ruled that Marbury had a right to the commission and deserved a remedy.
  • However, the part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 that gave the Court original jurisdiction to issue the writ conflicted with Article III of the Constitution.
  • The decision established judicial review: the Supreme Court may declare laws unconstitutional.

Other major Marshall Court decisions

Case Ruling Significance
Fletcher v. Peck (1810) Georgia could not cancel a valid land contract. Protected contracts and allowed the Court to invalidate a state law.
Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819) New Hampshire could not alter Dartmouth's private charter. Protected private contracts from state interference.
McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) Congress could create a national bank; Maryland could not tax it. Confirmed implied powers and federal supremacy.
Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) Congress controls interstate commerce. Expanded federal authority over the national economy.

Judicial impeachment

  • The House impeached Supreme Court Justice Samuel Chase, but the Senate did not remove him.
  • His acquittal helped protect judicial independence from partisan removal.

3. Jefferson's Presidency

Louisiana Purchase (1803)

  • Jefferson bought the Louisiana Territory from France for about $15 million, doubling the country's size.
  • The Constitution did not explicitly authorize the president to purchase territory, so Jefferson used a loose interpretation of the treaty-making power despite his usual preference for strict construction.
  • The Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806) mapped the region, studied its resources, and strengthened U.S. claims to the West.

Barbary Wars

  • North African states demanded tribute from countries whose ships used the Mediterranean.
  • Jefferson sent the Navy after Tripoli demanded more money.
  • Fighting from 1801 to 1805 demonstrated that the United States would use force to protect its trade.

Challenges to neutrality

  • Britain and France interfered with American trade during the Napoleonic Wars.
  • Britain practiced impressment, forcing sailors—including Americans—into the Royal Navy.
  • In the Chesapeake–Leopard Affair (1807), a British ship attacked the USS Chesapeake, killing three Americans and taking four sailors.

Embargo Act of 1807

  • Prohibited American ships from trading with foreign ports.
  • Jefferson hoped economic pressure would force Britain and France to respect U.S. neutrality.
  • Instead, the embargo badly harmed American merchants and encouraged smuggling.
  • It also stimulated domestic manufacturing by reducing access to foreign goods.

4. Madison and the War of 1812

Failed attempts at economic pressure

  • Non-Intercourse Act (1809): reopened trade with every nation except Britain and France.
  • Macon's Bill No. 2 (1810): promised to stop trade with one belligerent if the other respected U.S. neutral rights.
  • Napoleon falsely claimed that France would comply, leading Madison to restrict trade with Britain.

Causes of the War of 1812

  • British impressment of American sailors.
  • British interference with neutral American trade.
  • British support for Native resistance in the Northwest.
  • Pressure from War Hawks, including Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun.
  • Some Americans hoped to conquer Canada and remove Britain from North America.

Important events and results

  • The British burned Washington, D.C., in 1814.
  • The Treaty of Ghent, signed December 24, 1814, restored prewar boundaries but did not resolve impressment.
  • The Battle of New Orleans was fought on January 8, 1815, after the treaty was signed but before news reached the armies.
  • Andrew Jackson's victory made him a national hero.
  • The Hartford Convention (1814–1815) proposed constitutional amendments protecting New England's interests. Federalist talk of resistance and possible secession made the party appear disloyal after the war.

Consequences of the war

  • Increased nationalism and confidence in the United States.
  • Weakened Native resistance east of the Mississippi.
  • Encouraged American manufacturing.
  • Destroyed the Federalist Party as a national political force.
  • Elevated Andrew Jackson and other military leaders.

5. Nationalism and the Era of Good Feelings

American System

Henry Clay promoted a program to unite and strengthen the national economy:

  1. Protective tariff to support American manufacturing.
  2. Second Bank of the United States to stabilize currency and credit.
  3. Internal improvements such as roads and canals to connect regions.
  • The Tariff of 1816 was the first protective tariff in U.S. history.
  • The Second Bank of the United States was chartered in 1816.
  • The Panic of 1819, caused by tightening credit, land speculation, falling crop prices, and bank failures, created distrust of banks and national economic policy.

Monroe's foreign policy

  • Rush–Bagot Agreement (1817): limited naval forces on the Great Lakes.
  • Convention of 1818: set the U.S.–Canada boundary at the 49th parallel from the Lake of the Woods to the Rocky Mountains and provided joint occupation of Oregon.
  • Adams–Onís Treaty (1819): Spain ceded Florida to the United States; the United States gave up claims to Texas and accepted a boundary with Spanish territory.
  • Monroe Doctrine (1823): warned European powers against new colonization or intervention in the Western Hemisphere. In return, the United States would avoid European political conflicts.

Missouri Compromise (1820)

  • Admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, preserving the Senate balance.
  • Prohibited slavery in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of 36°30′, except Missouri.
  • Temporarily reduced sectional conflict but revealed that westward expansion would repeatedly reopen the slavery question.

6. The Market Revolution

Transportation and communication

  • National Road: connected the East with western settlements.
  • Erie Canal (1825): linked the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and made New York City the nation's leading commercial center.
  • Steamboats: made river travel faster and allowed movement upstream.
  • Railroads: expanded rapidly in the 1830s and 1840s.
  • Telegraph: Samuel Morse's system accelerated long-distance communication in the 1840s.

Industrialization and labor

  • Factories replaced some household production.
  • The Lowell System employed young women in textile mills under supervised living conditions.
  • Workers increasingly performed wage labor and began forming early labor unions.
  • Interchangeable parts and mechanization increased production.

Regional specialization

  • Northeast: manufacturing and commerce.
  • South: cotton production supported by enslaved labor.
  • West: commercial farming that supplied eastern cities.
  • Eli Whitney's cotton gin, invented in 1793, made short-staple cotton profitable and contributed to the expansion of slavery across the Deep South.

Social effects

  • A larger middle class developed, but inequality also increased.
  • Immigration, especially from Ireland and Germany in the 1840s, supplied labor and increased nativism.
  • The ideal of separate spheres placed men in public economic life and middle-class women in the domestic sphere, even as factory work and reform gave women new public roles.

7. Jacksonian Democracy

Election of 1824: the “Corrupt Bargain”

  • Andrew Jackson won a plurality of the popular and electoral votes but not an electoral majority.
  • The House chose among the top three candidates under the Twelfth Amendment.
  • House Speaker Henry Clay supported John Quincy Adams, who won and then appointed Clay secretary of state.
  • Jackson's supporters called this a “corrupt bargain,” though no deal was proven.

Election of 1828

  • Jackson defeated Adams in a bitter, highly personal campaign.
  • The election marked the growth of mass political parties and broader voting participation among white men as property requirements declined.
  • This expansion of democracy excluded women, Native Americans, and most African Americans.

Jackson's presidency

  • Used the spoils system, rewarding political supporters with government positions.
  • Claimed to represent the “common man,” while using presidential veto power more aggressively than earlier presidents.
  • The Democrats supported limited federal economic power and Jackson.
  • The Whigs opposed “King Andrew,” favored Congress, and supported economic modernization.

Nullification Crisis

  • Southern critics called the Tariff of 1828 the “Tariff of Abominations.”
  • Vice President John C. Calhoun argued that a state could nullify an unconstitutional federal law.
  • South Carolina nullified the tariffs of 1828 and 1832.
  • Jackson supported the Force Bill, while Henry Clay helped secure the Compromise Tariff of 1833.
  • The crisis ended peacefully but left unresolved the conflict between federal authority and states' rights.

Indian Removal

  • The Indian Removal Act (1830) authorized removal of Native nations east of the Mississippi to lands in present-day Oklahoma.
  • Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831): the Court ruled that the Cherokee were a “domestic dependent nation,” not a foreign nation able to sue under that part of the Constitution.
  • Worcester v. Georgia (1832): Georgia laws had no force within Cherokee territory.
  • State and federal authorities did not protect Cherokee sovereignty.
  • Under President Martin Van Buren, the Army forced about 15,000 Cherokee west in 1838–1839. Roughly 4,000 died on the Trail of Tears.

Bank War and Panic of 1837

  • Jackson believed the Second Bank favored wealthy elites and threatened liberty.
  • He vetoed its recharter in 1832 and removed federal deposits, placing them in state “pet banks.”
  • The Specie Circular (1836) required payment for federal land in gold or silver.
  • Bank instability, land speculation, falling cotton prices, British credit restrictions, and the Specie Circular contributed to the Panic of 1837.

8. The Second Great Awakening and Reform

Second Great Awakening

  • Protestant revival movement emphasizing free will, personal salvation, and moral improvement.
  • Camp meetings expanded religion on the frontier.
  • The belief that individuals could improve themselves encouraged efforts to perfect society.

Major reform movements

Movement Leaders or examples Goal
Temperance American Temperance Society Reduce alcohol consumption.
Public education Horace Mann Tax-supported schools and trained teachers.
Asylum and prison reform Dorothea Dix Humane treatment for people with mental illness and incarcerated people.
Abolition William Lloyd Garrison, Frederick Douglass, the Grimké sisters Immediate or gradual end to slavery, depending on the organization.
Women's rights Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Lucretia Mott Legal, political, and economic equality.

Seneca Falls Convention (1848)

  • The Declaration of Sentiments modeled its language on the Declaration of Independence.
  • Declared that “all men and women are created equal.”
  • Demanded expanded legal rights, educational and economic opportunities, and women's suffrage.

Utopian and cultural movements

  • Communities such as Brook Farm, New Harmony, and the Oneida Community attempted alternatives to competitive market society.
  • Transcendentalists, including Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau, emphasized individual conscience, self-reliance, and nature.
  • American writers and artists developed a more distinct national culture.

9. Westward Expansion

  • Population growth, cheap land, transportation improvements, and the search for economic opportunity encouraged western settlement.
  • Expansion increased conflict with Native nations and accelerated their dispossession.
  • Americans traveled west along routes such as the Oregon Trail.
  • Texas declared independence from Mexico in 1836 and was annexed by the United States in 1845.
  • Expansion repeatedly raised the question of whether slavery would spread into new territories.

10. High-Value APUSH Connections

Theme Evidence Main argument
Federal power increased Marshall Court, American System, Force Bill Even leaders who praised limited government sometimes expanded national authority.
Democracy expanded and remained limited White male suffrage, mass parties, Indian Removal, slavery Political participation broadened mainly for white men while other groups faced exclusion or removal.
The economy became more connected Canals, railroads, factories, telegraph The Market Revolution linked regions but also increased inequality and sectional specialization.
Westward expansion intensified conflict Louisiana Purchase, Missouri Compromise, Indian Removal, Texas Expansion increased opportunity for settlers while displacing Native peoples and worsening conflict over slavery.
Reform grew from religious change Second Great Awakening, temperance, abolition, women's rights Belief in individual moral improvement encouraged attempts to reform society.
Sectionalism grew beneath nationalism Missouri Compromise, Tariff of 1828, Nullification Crisis National unity after the War of 1812 coexisted with deepening regional divisions.

11. Essential Dates

  • 1800: Jefferson elected; peaceful party transfer.
  • 1803: Marbury v. Madison; Louisiana Purchase.
  • 1807: Chesapeake–Leopard Affair; Embargo Act.
  • 1812–1815: War of 1812.
  • 1819: McCulloch v. Maryland; Adams–Onís Treaty; Panic of 1819.
  • 1820: Missouri Compromise.
  • 1823: Monroe Doctrine.
  • 1824: Gibbons v. Ogden; disputed presidential election.
  • 1828: Jackson elected; Tariff of Abominations.
  • 1830: Indian Removal Act.
  • 1832–1833: Nullification Crisis; Bank veto.
  • 1837: Panic of 1837.
  • 1838–1839: Trail of Tears.
  • 1848: Seneca Falls Convention; end of APUSH Period 4.

12. Fast Review Questions

  1. How did Jefferson's actions sometimes conflict with his belief in limited federal power?
  2. Why did Marbury v. Madison strengthen the Supreme Court even though Marbury lost?
  3. What caused the War of 1812, and how did it change American politics and economics?
  4. How did the Market Revolution connect the regions while also increasing sectionalism?
  5. In what ways did Jacksonian democracy expand political participation, and whom did it exclude?
  6. How did the Second Great Awakening contribute to antebellum reform?
  7. Why did westward expansion intensify disputes over Native sovereignty and slavery?
