Part I · Multiple choice (2 points)#

1. Four unknown food samples (W, X, Y, Z) were each tested with IKI, Benedict's reagent (heated) and Biuret reagent. The results are shown below.

Sample IKI solution Benedict's (after heating) Biuret reagent
W blue-black brick red purple
X yellow-brown brick red purple
Y yellow-brown blue purple
Z yellow-brown brick red light blue

Which sample contains glucose and protein but no starch?

(A) W (B) X (C) Y (D) Z

Part II · Structure and function (15 points)#

2. (4 points) The diagram shows oxaloacetate, the four-carbon acid produced when CO₂ is first fixed in the mesophyll cells of a C4 plant.

(a) Circle two different functional groups on the molecule and name each one. (2)

(b) Oxaloacetate dissolves readily in the watery cytoplasm of the cell. Using the functional groups you circled, explain why. (2)

3. (6 points) The diagram is a cross-section of a leaf from a C4 plant. Six structures are labelled A to F.

(a) Give the letter and name of the tissue whose cells make the enzyme RuBisCO. (1)

(b) Give the letter and name of the tissue whose cells make the enzyme maltase. (1)

(c) Name the ring-shaped arrangement of cells around each vascular bundle, and state what this type of leaf anatomy is called. (2)

(d) Describe the environmental conditions that favour the evolution of C4 photosynthesis, and explain why. (2)

4. (5 points) Read the passage.

In 1920 Otto Warburg noticed that when he raised the oxygen concentration around illuminated algae, they fixed less carbon dioxide. Fifty years later, Bowes, Ogren and Hageman showed why: the enzyme RuBisCO can bind O₂ in place of CO₂. When it does, the Calvin cycle is "poisoned" with a two-carbon product (phosphoglycolate) that the plant has to break down in a series of reactions that consume ATP and release CO₂. In hot, bright, dry conditions this side reaction can waste a quarter of the carbon a C3 plant fixes.

(a) Name the process the scientists were describing. (1)

(b) Explain, with reference to RuBisCO, why this process is harmful to a plant. Give at least three distinct reasons. (4)

Part III · Data analysis (28 points)#

5. (8 points) A student measured the rate of photosynthesis of Elodea at 25 °C across a range of light intensities, first in normal air (0.04% CO₂) and then in CO₂-enriched air (0.4% CO₂).

(a) Identify the factor limiting the rate of photosynthesis at the right-hand end of each curve (above light intensity 6). Explain how you can tell from the graph. (2)

(b) Identify the factor limiting the rate at the beginning of both curves (light intensity 0 to 1). Explain how you can tell. (2)

(c) Calculate the percent difference between the maximum rates reached at 0.4% CO₂ and 0.04% CO₂. Show your working. (2)

(d) Explain why a percent difference is a better comparison here than simply subtracting one maximum rate from the other. (2)

6. (8 points) Proteins were extracted from leaves of four plant species (A to D) and separated by gel electrophoresis alongside a size marker (M). A separate pigment extract from one leaf was separated by paper chromatography.

(a) Identify the two species with the most similar protein and justify your choice using the gel. (2)

(b) The chromatogram below shows one pigment spot. Calculate its R_f value. Show your working. (2)

(c) Explain two reasons why gel electrophoresis is a more useful technique than paper chromatography for comparing the proteins of different species. (4)

7. (12 points) Students wanted to know whether a heat wave changes the transpiration rate of Hibiscus rosa-sinensis. Nine potted plants of the same size were placed in potometers. Each plant's water loss was measured for one hour under normal conditions (28 °C) and again during a heat wave (38 °C).

(a) Complete the table by calculating the standard error and the 95% confidence interval for each condition. Show your working for one condition. (4)

Answer key (open after attempting)

1. (B) X. Only X is Benedict's-positive (brick red: reducing sugar, i.e. glucose), Biuret-positive (purple: protein) and IKI-negative (yellow-brown: no starch). W also has starch, Y has no reducing sugar, Z has no protein.

2. (a) Carboxyl (–COOH, there are two: one at each end) and ketone / carbonyl (C=O on the second carbon). (b) Both groups are polar: the O–H and C=O bonds carry partial charges, so they form hydrogen bonds with water. Oxaloacetate is therefore hydrophilic and dissolves in the cytoplasm (at cell pH the carboxyl groups are ionised to –COO⁻, which makes it even more soluble).

3. (a) C, bundle sheath cells. In C4 plants the Calvin cycle (and so RuBisCO) is confined to the bundle sheath, away from the O₂ made by the light reactions in the mesophyll. (b) Maltase: not in my notes, so check this one. Best guess is B, mesophyll cells, where starch is stored and broken down to maltose and then glucose. (c) Bundle sheath ring; Kranz anatomy ("wreath"). (d) Hot, dry, high-light environments (tropical grasses such as maize and sugarcane). Heat makes stomata close to save water, so CO₂ falls and O₂ builds up inside the leaf, RuBisCO starts binding O₂ (photorespiration), and the C4 CO₂-pump keeps RuBisCO saturated with CO₂ instead. Other labels: A cuticle/upper epidermis, D vascular bundle (xylem, with phloem below), E lower epidermis, F guard cell of a stoma.

4. (a) Photorespiration. (b) RuBisCO's active site accepts O₂ as well as CO₂, so when O₂ is high and CO₂ is low it acts as an oxygenase instead of a carboxylase. Then: no sugar (PGAL) is made from that turnover; the 2-carbon phosphoglycolate is toxic and must be recycled in reactions that use ATP; the recycling releases CO₂ that was already fixed, so net carbon gain falls; and the plant's growth, yield and ability to survive heat drop as a result.

5. (a) Both curves are flat, so light is no longer limiting. For the 0.04% curve the limiting factor is CO₂ concentration (raising CO₂ to 0.4% lifts the plateau, which proves it). For the 0.4% curve it is temperature (or enzyme/RuBisCO capacity), since CO₂ and light are both plentiful. (b) Light intensity: both curves rise steeply together and the slope is the same regardless of CO₂, so adding CO₂ makes no difference at low light. (c) Maxima ≈ 40 and 16 bubbles min⁻¹. % difference = |40 − 16| ÷ ((40 + 16)/2) × 100 = 24 ÷ 28 × 100 = 85.71%. (d) A percent difference is relative: it tells you how big the change is compared with the size of the measurements, it has no units, and it lets you compare this result with experiments that used different plants, apparatus or units. A raw difference of 24 bubbles means nothing on its own.

6. (a) B and D: identical band pattern (75, 50 and 25 kDa). A shares two bands with them, C shares one. Same-sized proteins migrate the same distance. (b) R_f = 6.4 cm ÷ 8.0 cm = 0.80. (c) Any two: electrophoresis separates by size and charge, giving sharper, more reproducible bands than diffuse chromatography spots; it runs many samples side by side on one gel against a size marker, so species can be compared directly and molecular mass estimated; it works on proteins and DNA, which do not move on paper the way small coloured pigments do, and bands can be stained or blotted afterwards for further analysis.

7. (a) Normal: SE = 0.23 ÷ √9 = 0.23 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.08; 95% CI = 2.24 ± 0.16 ≈ 2.08 to 2.40. Heat wave: SE = 0.35 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.12; 95% CI = 3.63 ± 0.24 ≈ 3.39 to 3.87 (accept small rounding differences). (b) Two bars at 2.24 and 3.63 with error bars reaching the CI limits above; axes labelled with units, title names the organism and variables. (c) The heat wave significantly increased transpiration: the mean rose from 2.24 to 3.63 mL h⁻¹ (about 62%) and the 95% CI error bars do not overlap, so the difference is unlikely to be due to chance. Biologically, higher temperature increases evaporation from the spongy mesophyll and steepens the water-potential gradient to the air, so water is pulled through the xylem faster; if the heat were extreme enough the stomata would close and the rate would eventually fall, which would be a good follow-up to test. Limitations worth one line: only 9 plants, one hour of data, humidity and wind not controlled.

Topics that keep coming up#

  • Statistics chain: SD → SE → 95% CI → "do the bars overlap?" (Q7, and Lab 1)
  • RuBisCO as oxygenase: photorespiration, why C4 and Kranz anatomy exist (Q3, Q4)
  • Reading limiting-factor graphs and then doing a calculation on them (Q5)
  • The three biomolecule colour tests, including negative results (Q1)
  • Separation techniques: what each one separates by and how to read the output (Q6)

Question formats to expect#

  • Circle and name functional groups on a molecule model (Q2)
  • Label tissues on a leaf cross-section and name the structure (Q3)
  • Short explanation FRQ from a passage (Q4)
  • Read a two-line graph for limiting factors, then a percent difference calculation (Q5)
  • Interpret a gel, calculate R_f, compare the two methods (Q6)
  • Statistics FRQ: SE and 95% CI calculation, bar graph with error bars, written conclusion (Q7)
  • MCQ reading results of the three biomolecule tests (Q1)

Still unsure about#

  • Where maltase is made in a C4 leaf (Q3b): not in my notes. Ask or look up before the test.
  • Which percent difference formula the class uses (difference over the average, or over the smaller value). Check the worksheet.
  • Whether Q7(b) wants a bar graph or a true box plot. The test said "box plot with error lines", which in this class has meant bars with 95% CI whiskers.
