Honors Biology — Test 2 Master Notes: Energy and Photosynthesis

← Exams & Reviews · Unit 2 hub · Concept map (canvas) · Class home

[!abstract] The whole unit in seven sentences
1. Almost all energy in an ecosystem enters as sunlight captured by autotrophs.
2. Light that hits a leaf is reflected, transmitted or absorbed; only absorbed light does work, and only a few percent ends up stored in sugar.
3. Pigments in the thylakoid membrane absorb blue-violet and red light; the energy excites an electron in chlorophyll a.
4. In the light reactions, two photosystems pass those electrons from water to NADP⁺, releasing O₂ and building an H⁺ gradient that makes ATP.
5. In the Calvin–Benson cycle, ATP and NADPH are used to fix CO₂ into glucose.
6. Glucose is stored as starch, built into cellulose, or burned in cellular respiration; eaten, it passes up the food chain with only about 10% surviving each step.
7. Getting CO₂ in means opening stomata, which lets water out: the plant is always balancing the two.

Sources: my notebook (21 pages), the Photosynthesis slide deck (65 slides), the teacher's tentative Test 2 outline. Each section links to a fuller note.

1. Energy in ecosystems

Full notes: Autotrophs vs Heterotrophs · Food Chains and Trophic Levels · Energy Pyramid and the 10 Percent Rule

1.1 Autotrophs and heterotrophs

Autotroph (producer) Heterotroph (consumer)
Carbon source CO₂ (inorganic) Other organisms (organic)
Energy source Light Food
Photosynthesis Yes No
Cellular respiration Yes Yes
Examples Plants, algae, cyanobacteria, phytoplankton, Euglena Paramecium, shrimp, fish, birds, humans
  • Photoautotrophs make their own organic matter from CO₂ and water using light. Not only plants: some bacteria and protists too.
  • Autotrophs still do cellular respiration. They make glucose and burn it.
  • Heterotrophs lose energy as heat and pass out what they cannot digest (cellulose, fiber).

1.2 Food chains

  • A food chain shows how organisms are related to each other by the food they eat. Arrows point the way the energy goes.
  • Notebook chain (pond): algae (producer) → paramecium (primary consumer) → small predator (secondary) → shrimp (tertiary) → fish (quaternary) → birds (5th).
  • Slide chain (land): grass → grasshopper → mouse → snake → hawk.
  • A food web is many chains linked together.

1.3 The energy pyramid

  • Each step up a trophic level, only about 10% of the biomass is passed on.
  • Biomass = stored chemical energy, so the pyramid of biomass is also the pyramid of energy.
  • Where the 90% goes: (1) indigestible material such as cellulose leaves as feces; (2) most digested food is burned for energy and lost as heat; (3) some parts are never eaten.
  • Pattern: 100% → 10% → 1% → 0.1% → 0.01%. Energy at level n = producers × 0.1^(n−1).
  • Energy flows one way and ends as heat. It is not recycled.

2. Light and the leaf

Full notes: Light and the Electromagnetic Spectrum · Energy Efficiency of Photosynthesis · Photosynthetic Pigments · Chlorophyll Excitation and Fluorescence

2.1 Light is energy

  • Sunlight travels as photons; it is measured in wavelengths.
  • Visible light ≈ 380–750 nm, the middle of the electromagnetic spectrum. The atmosphere screens out most of the rest.
  • Energy is inversely related to wavelength. Shorter λ = higher energy. Violet and blue carry more energy per photon than red.
  • Colors in order of decreasing wavelength: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
  • The color we see = sunlight minus the wavelengths absorbed. We see what is reflected or transmitted.

2.2 What happens to light that hits a leaf

Three possibilities: reflected, transmitted, absorbed. Leaves reflect and transmit green, which is why they look green from both sides. Only absorbed light can power photosynthesis.

Step Left Lost because
Sunlight hitting the leaf 100%
Usable wavelengths (400–700 nm) ~53% UV and infrared are no use to the pigments
Absorbed by pigments ~37% reflected or transmitted (mostly green)
Captured by chlorophyll ~28% heat
Stored as sugar ~9% cost of the light reactions and Calvin cycle
Net after the leaf's own respiration ~5% respiration, photorespiration

[!warning] Numbers not from class
My notes and the slides cover this topic only in words. The percentages are standard textbook values (Hall & Rao). The point to be able to explain: most light energy is never stored; it is the wrong wavelength, bounces off, passes through, or becomes heat (second law of thermodynamics).

Energy transformations in order: light (radiant) energy → excited electrons → chemical energy in ATP and NADPH → chemical potential energy in the bonds of glucose → ATP again in respiration → heat.

2.3 Pigments

A pigment is a substance that absorbs visible light. Different pigments absorb different wavelengths.

Pigment Color Role
Chlorophyll a blue-green The only pigment that participates directly in the light reactions
Chlorophyll b yellow-green Accessory (antenna): absorbs light, passes energy to chlorophyll a
Carotenoids yellow, orange Accessory
Xanthophylls yellow (slide says purples) Accessory
Anthocyanin red, pink, purple (slide says yellow) Water-soluble; in the vacuole; not part of the photosystems

[!warning] Ask the teacher
Slide 21 has xanthophylls as "purples" and anthocyanin as "yellow". Standard biology, and my own Activity 10 notes ("pink from anthocyanin"), say the reverse. Find out which the test expects.

  • Chlorophyll structure: a porphyrin ring (delocalized electrons that absorb light) + a hydrophobic phytol tail (anchors it in the thylakoid membrane). Chlorophyll a has a methyl group where chlorophyll b has a carbonyl group.
  • Why accessory pigments? They broaden the range of wavelengths the plant can use.

2.4 Absorption spectrum vs. action spectrum

Absorption spectrum Action spectrum
Plots Light absorbed by a pigment vs. wavelength Rate of photosynthesis vs. wavelength
Tool Spectrophotometer Measure O₂ output under each color
Key feature Chlorophyll a: sharp peaks in blue-violet and red, almost nothing in green Broader; does not fall to zero in green
  • The action spectrum is wider than chlorophyll a's absorption spectrum because accessory pigments pass on energy from wavelengths chlorophyll a misses.
  • Engelmann: lit an algal filament with a spectrum; oxygen-loving bacteria gathered at the violet-blue and red parts, where most O₂ was produced.
  • Spectrophotometer: light source → prism → slit picks one wavelength → sample → detector.

2.5 Excited chlorophyll

  • A photon lifts a chlorophyll electron from ground state to an excited state.
  • In a photosystem: an electron-accepting protein (the primary electron acceptor) picks it up. Energy is kept.
  • Alone in solution: nothing accepts it, so it falls back. Energy is re-emitted as heat + longer-wavelength (red) light = fluorescence.
  • Red, not blue, because some energy is always lost as heat first (second law of thermodynamics), and less energy means longer wavelength.

3. The photosynthesis machine

Full notes: Photosynthesis Overview · Chloroplast Structure · Photosystems · Light Reactions · Z Scheme · ATP and NADPH · Calvin-Benson Cycle

3.1 Equation and overview

$$6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O + \text{light energy} \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2$$

Three major events: (1) sunlight is converted into chemical energy; (2) water is split and O₂ released; (3) CO₂ is fixed into sugar.

Needs light? Yes, directly Not directly, but stops without the light reactions' products
  • The O₂ comes from water, not CO₂.
  • Glucose is used for cellular respiration, cellulose, starch and other organic compounds.

3.2 Chloroplast

  • Thylakoid: flattened sac; its membrane holds the pigments. Granum: a stack of thylakoids. Stroma: the fluid outside the thylakoids. Lumen: the space inside a thylakoid.
  • Thylakoids are highly folded and stacked to increase surface area for absorbing light.
  • Zoom path: leaf → mesophyll cell → chloroplast → granum → thylakoid.

3.3 Photosystems

A photosystem is a complex of pigments and proteins for capturing light energy in the thylakoid membrane.

  • Antenna pigments (several hundred chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and accessory pigments) pass energy inward like a game of hot potato.
  • The reaction center (chlorophyll a + primary electron acceptor) is where an electron is actually handed over.
  • Why: to harvest light over a larger surface area and a larger part of the spectrum than one pigment could.
PSII PSI
Reaction center P680 P700
Position First Second
Electrons replaced by Splitting water (O₂ released) Electrons arriving from PSII
Leads to ETC → H⁺ gradient → ATP NADP⁺ reductase → NADPH

3.4 Light reactions

  1. Photolysis at PSII: 2 H₂O → 4 H⁺ + O₂ + 4 e⁻.
  2. Electrons enter the electron transport chain: PSII → plastoquinone → cytochrome complex → plastocyanin → PSI.
  3. Plastoquinone and the cytochrome complex move H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen.
  4. PSI re-excites the electrons → ferredoxin → ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase.
  5. NADP⁺ + H⁺ + 2 e⁻ → NADPH (for the dark reactions).
  6. Chemiosmosis: high H⁺ in the lumen flows out through ATP synthase: ADP + Pᵢ → ATP.

3.5 Z scheme

  • A graph of electron energy (y) against the sequence of carriers (x).
  • Two rises = two photons absorbed (P680, then P700).
  • First fall (acceptor → Pq → cytochrome → Pc) releases energy that pumps H⁺ → ATP.
  • Second fall (acceptor → Fd → NADP⁺ reductase) ends in NADPH.
  • Electrons start in water and end in NADPH.

3.6 Noncyclic vs. cyclic electron flow

Noncyclic Cyclic
Photosystems II and I I only
Path H₂O → PSII → ETC → PSI → NADP⁺ PSI → Fd → cytochrome → Pc → back to PSI
ATP Yes Yes
NADPH Yes No
O₂ Yes No

Why cyclic: the Calvin cycle needs more ATP than NADPH (18 vs. 12 per glucose). ATP is the limiting factor, and cyclic flow fills the deficit.

3.7 ATP, NADPH and redox

ATP NADPH
Carries Energy in phosphate bonds High-energy electrons (+ H)
Empty form ADP + Pᵢ NADP⁺
Releases its load by Losing the third phosphate Giving electrons to the Calvin cycle
  • ATP = adenosine (adenine + ribose) + three phosphates. "Cellular currency."
  • Reduction = gain of electrons (escorted by hydrogens). Oxidation = loss of electrons. OIL RIG.
  • In photosynthesis overall: water is oxidized, CO₂ is reduced.

3.8 Calvin–Benson cycle

Phase What happens
1. Carbon fixation RuBisCO joins CO₂ to RuBP (5 C) → unstable 6 C → 2 PGA (3 C)
2. Reduction ATP and NADPH turn PGA into PGAL (G3P); NADP⁺ and ADP go back to the light reactions
3. Regeneration Most PGAL is rebuilt into RuBP using ATP, so the cycle continues
  • Carbon fixation = inorganic carbon (CO₂) → organic molecule.
  • Per glucose: 6 turns, 6 CO₂ + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH. Carbon count: 6 RuBP (30 C) + 6 CO₂ (6 C) = 36 C → 12 PGAL; 2 leave as glucose (6 C), 10 regenerate 6 RuBP (30 C).
  • It does not need light itself, but it cannot continue in the dark because the ATP and NADPH run out.

4. Sugar chemistry and the two tests

Full notes: Carbohydrates and Glycosidic Bonds · Activity 8 - IKI Test for Starch · Activity 9 - Benedict Test for Reducing Sugars

4.1 Building and breaking carbohydrates

  • Carbohydrates: Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ. Glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆. In α-glucose the –OH on carbon 1 points down.
  • Dehydration synthesis (condensation): glucose + glucose → maltose + H₂O. Forms a glycosidic bond (covalent, C–O–C).
  • Hydrolysis: the reverse; adds H₂O to split the bond.
  • Photosynthesis stores solar energy as potential energy in glucose's chemical bonds.
Carbohydrate Type Role
Glucose Monosaccharide Fuel
Maltose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) Disaccharide: glucose + glucose From starch breakdown
Sucrose Disaccharide: glucose + fructose Non-reducing
Amylose (starch) Polysaccharide Plant energy storage
Cellulose Polysaccharide Cell wall; fiber

4.2 IKI and Benedict's

IKI Benedict's
Detects Starch Reducing sugars
Amount used in class 5 drops 10 drops, heated at 80 °C
Negative Yellow-orange (IKI's own color) Blue
Positive Blue-black Green → yellow → orange → red
Glucose − (light orange) + (light orange)
Maltose − (lighter orange) + (darker orange)
Amylose + (dark purple / blue / black, opaque) very weak (yellow-green)
dH₂O (control) − (lightest orange) − (still light blue)
  • Why IKI works: hydrogen bonds between –OH groups coil amylose into a helix; iodine is trapped in the helix and looks dark blue. Add amylase → maltose, and the blue goes.
  • Why Benedict's works: a reducing sugar has a free reactive carbon that can open to C=O and give electrons to Cu²⁺. Glucose and maltose have one. Sucrose has none (both are locked in the glycosidic bond). Amylose has one per very long chain.
  • The sugar is oxidized; the copper is reduced.

Activity 7: light and pigments

  • Room light: extract is dark green. Dark room: black. Lamp in a dark room: green from the top, red from the bottom.
  • Green = reflected. Red = fluorescence (section 2.5).
  • Each extract absorbs the high-energy violet end; the vegetable's color is what is not absorbed.
  • Ethyl alcohol = control (absorbs nothing). Sunlight = full spectrum for comparison.

Activity 10: pigments and energy transformation in a leaf

  • Leaf has green (chlorophyll), pink (anthocyanin) and purple (both) areas.
  • Hot water, then hot ethanol (heated in a water bath because ethanol is flammable). Ethanol disrupts the membranes, the pigment dissolves, the solution turns green and the leaf goes white.
  • Iodine: black / dark purple = starch = that tissue was photosynthesizing. White = no starch.
  • Is chlorophyll needed? Yes: only green areas made starch.
  • Is light needed? Expected yes: a leaf in the dark makes no new starch. My first trial did not show it (the dark leaf still had starch); trial 2 showed less starch in the dark leaf.
  • Why: starch is energy storage. When glucose runs out the plant hydrolyzes starch to glucose and makes ATP by cellular respiration to maintain homeostasis, so starch disappears gradually.

6. Leaf, stomata and water (lab)

Full notes: Lab - Water Retention in Plants · Stomata and Transpiration · Leaf Structure and Water Transport

Stomata

  • Stoma = pore controlled by two guard cells. Guard cells are green (chloroplasts); other epidermal cells are transparent.
  • CO₂ in, O₂ and H₂O vapor out.
  • Opening and closing is controlled by light and water: plenty of water → guard cells swell → open; short of water → limp → closed.
  • Land plants: more stomata on the underside, so water leaves more slowly. Floating water plants: the opposite.

Part 1: percent mass change

$$\%\ \text{mass change} = \frac{\text{final} - \text{initial}}{\text{initial}} \times 100$$

  • Negative = mass lost = water lost by transpiration. Percent lets you compare leaves of different sizes.
  • Faster water loss: larger leaf (more surface area), thinner cuticle and wax, more open stomata.
  • Slower: thick waxy cuticle, fewer or closed stomata.

Moving water and food

  • Veins bring water from roots to leaf and move nutrients where they are needed.
  • Water goes one way (xylem). Nutrients go both ways (phloem).
  • Water rises because of cohesion, adhesion, evaporation from the leaves, and root pressure.
  • Monocot: parallel veins. Dicot: net-like veins.

Photorespiration, C4 and CAM (slides 60–63)

Full note: Photorespiration, C4 and CAM Plants

  • Hot, dry day → stomata close → CO₂ falls, O₂ rises → RuBisCO binds O₂ → uses ATP, releases CO₂, no sugar.
  • C4 plants: spatial separation. PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ into a 4 C compound in mesophyll cells; the Calvin cycle runs in bundle-sheath cells.
  • CAM plants: temporal separation. CO₂ is fixed at night and stored as an acid in the vacuole; the Calvin cycle runs in the day with stomata shut.

Glycolysis and cellular respiration (notebook)

Full note: Glycolysis and Cellular Respiration

  • Respiration is the reverse equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ATP.
  • Glycolysis: in the cytoplasm of all cells. Glucose (6 C) + 2 ATP → 4 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 pyruvate (3 C). Net 2 ATP.
  • Investment phase (phosphates added using ATP; making fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is the rate-determining step), then generation phase (split into two glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate → pyruvate).
  • Pyruvate needs O₂ to go on to the mitochondria.

Amphipathic molecules (notebook)

Full note: Amphipathic Molecules and Membranes

  • Amphipathic = has hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.
  • Ethanol: polar –OH + non-polar carbon end. Phospholipid: hydrophilic phosphate head + hydrophobic tails.
  • Ethanol disrupts the thylakoid membrane, so membrane-bound pigments are released.

Mixed self-test

Cover the answers; click to check.

[!question]- 1. Producers in a field store 80,000 kJ. How much is available to tertiary consumers?
80,000 × 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 80 kJ.

[!question]- 2. Give three reasons only ~10% of energy moves up a trophic level.
Heat lost in cellular respiration; indigestible material (cellulose) lost as feces; parts of organisms not eaten.

[!question]- 3. Name the three fates of light striking a leaf. Which one powers photosynthesis?
Reflected, transmitted, absorbed. Only absorbed light.

[!question]- 4. Which has more energy per photon: 400 nm or 700 nm? Why?
400 nm. Energy is inversely related to wavelength.

[!question]- 5. Why are leaves green?
Chlorophyll absorbs blue-violet and red light and reflects and transmits green.

[!question]- 6. Why is the action spectrum broader than chlorophyll a's absorption spectrum?
Accessory pigments (chlorophyll b, carotenoids) absorb other wavelengths and transfer the energy to chlorophyll a.

[!question]- 7. A chlorophyll extract glows red under a lamp. A leaf does not. Explain.
In the extract there is no electron acceptor, so excited electrons fall back and emit red light and heat. In a chloroplast the primary electron acceptor takes the electron into the electron transport chain.

[!question]- 8. Where do the light reactions and the Calvin cycle happen?
Thylakoid membrane; stroma.

[!question]- 9. What is the source of the O₂ released in photosynthesis?
Water, split at PSII.

[!question]- 10. List the electron carriers from water to NADPH.
H₂O → PSII (P680) → primary acceptor → plastoquinone → cytochrome complex → plastocyanin → PSI (P700) → primary acceptor → ferredoxin → NADP⁺ reductase → NADPH.

[!question]- 11. Describe chemiosmosis in the chloroplast.
The electron transport chain pumps H⁺ into the thylakoid lumen. H⁺ then diffuses back to the stroma through ATP synthase, driving ADP + Pᵢ → ATP.

[!question]- 12. On the Z scheme, what do the two upward steps and the two downward slopes represent?
Up: light absorbed at PSII and PSI. Down: electrons releasing energy, first to make ATP, then ending in NADPH.

[!question]- 13. How is cyclic electron flow different, and why does the plant need it?
PSI only; electrons loop back through the cytochrome complex; ATP but no NADPH or O₂. It supplies the extra ATP the Calvin cycle needs.

[!question]- 14. Name the three phases of the Calvin cycle and what each uses.
Carbon fixation (CO₂, RuBisCO); reduction (ATP, NADPH); regeneration of RuBP (ATP).

[!question]- 15. How many CO₂, ATP and NADPH for one glucose?
6 CO₂, 18 ATP, 12 NADPH.

[!question]- 16. In photosynthesis, what is oxidized and what is reduced?
Water is oxidized; CO₂ is reduced.

[!question]- 17. A sample is orange after heating with Benedict's and yellow-orange with IKI. What does it contain?
A reducing sugar (such as glucose or maltose) and no starch.

[!question]- 18. Why does iodine turn starch blue-black?
Amylose coils into a helix (hydrogen bonds between –OH groups) and iodine is trapped inside.

[!question]- 19. Why is sucrose non-reducing?
Both reactive carbons are tied up in the glycosidic bond, so there is no free group to reduce Cu²⁺.

[!question]- 20. What reaction joins two glucose molecules, and what reaction separates them?
Dehydration synthesis (releases H₂O) joins them; hydrolysis (adds H₂O) separates them.

[!question]- 21. Which of Elodea, Cladophora, Spirogyra and Oscillatoria has no chloroplasts? Why?
Oscillatoria. It is a cyanobacterium, a prokaryote with no membrane-bound organelles.

[!question]- 22. Why were Elodea's chloroplasts at the edge of the cell and moving?
The central vacuole pushes them outward (more surface area for light); cytoplasmic streaming moves them.

[!question]- 23. Why boil a leaf in ethanol before the iodine test, and why in a water bath?
Ethanol disrupts membranes and removes chlorophyll so the starch color shows. Ethanol is flammable, so it is not heated on a flame.

[!question]- 24. A variegated leaf (green and white) is tested with iodine. Result and conclusion?
Only the green areas turn blue-black. Chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis.

[!question]- 25. A leaf kept in the dark for a short time still has starch. Why?
Starch is stored energy. It is only gradually hydrolyzed to glucose and used in respiration.

[!question]- 26. A leaf goes from 4.00 g to 3.40 g. Percent mass change?
(3.40 − 4.00) ÷ 4.00 × 100 = −15%.

[!question]- 27. Why are most stomata on the lower surface of land plants?
It is shaded and cooler there, so less water evaporates.

[!question]- 28. State the trade-off of opening stomata.
CO₂ can enter for the Calvin cycle, but water vapor is lost by transpiration.

[!question]- 29. What four things move water up a plant?
Cohesion, adhesion, evaporation from the leaves, root pressure.

[!question]- 30. What is photorespiration, and how do C4 and CAM plants avoid it?
RuBisCO binds O₂ instead of CO₂, wasting ATP and carbon. C4 separates CO₂ capture and the Calvin cycle in space; CAM separates them in time (night vs. day).
