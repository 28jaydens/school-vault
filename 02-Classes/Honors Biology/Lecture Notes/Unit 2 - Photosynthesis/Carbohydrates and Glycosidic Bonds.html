Carbohydrates and Glycosidic Bonds

← Unit 2 hub · Test 2 master notes · Class home

[!summary] Big idea
Photosynthesis stores solar energy as potential energy in the chemical bonds of glucose. Glucose units are joined by dehydration synthesis into bigger carbohydrates (maltose, starch, cellulose) and split apart again by hydrolysis. The structure of each carbohydrate explains how it behaves in the IKI and Benedict's tests.

Key Concepts

  • Carbohydrate: general formula Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ, "hydrated carbon".
  • Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆: a six-carbon ring sugar. In α-glucose the –OH on carbon 1 points down.
  • Glycosidic bond: the covalent C–O–C link between two sugars.
  • Dehydration synthesis (condensation): two sugars join and release H₂O.
  • Hydrolysis: add H₂O to break the bond. The reverse reaction.

$$C_6H_{12}O_6 + C_6H_{12}O_6 \;\rightleftharpoons\; C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O$$

Left to right is dehydration synthesis; right to left is hydrolysis.

Details and Evidence

The carbohydrates in this unit

Carbohydrate Type Made of Job
Glucose Monosaccharide one ring, C₆H₁₂O₆ Fuel; product of photosynthesis
Maltose Disaccharide glucose + glucose, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ What starch breaks into
Sucrose Disaccharide glucose + fructose Transport sugar in plants
Amylose (plant starch) Polysaccharide long chain of α-glucose Energy storage in plants
Cellulose Polysaccharide long chain of glucose Cell walls; "fiber", indigestible for most consumers

Why amylose coils, and why iodine turns it blue-black

  • Along the chain, the hanging –OH groups form hydrogen bonds with other parts of the same chain.
  • Those hydrogen bonds bend the chain until it coils into a helix. Because the –OH groups bond with each other instead of with water, amylose is insoluble.
  • Iodine gets trapped inside the helix, and the trapped iodine looks dark blue-black. That is the positive IKI test.
  • Glucose and maltose have no helix, so nothing traps the iodine and the solution stays IKI's own yellow-orange.
  • Amylose + iodine → blue. Add amylase (the enzyme that hydrolyzes starch) → maltose + iodine, and the blue disappears because the helix is gone.

Reducing vs. non-reducing sugars

  • A reducing sugar has a free reactive carbon (carbon 1) that can open up into a C=O group. That group can give electrons to Benedict's reagent.
  • Glucose and maltose have one: positive Benedict's.
  • Sucrose does not: both reactive carbons are used up in the glycosidic bond ("hidden O", C–O–C instead of C=O).
  • Amylose has only one free end on a chain of hundreds of glucoses, so it barely reacts.

Starch as an energy store

When glucose runs out (for example, a leaf kept in the dark), the plant breaks starch down by hydrolysis back to glucose, which is then turned into ATP by cellular respiration and used to maintain homeostasis. That is why the dark leaf had less starch in Activity 10.

Connections

  • Where glucose comes from: Calvin-Benson Cycle.
  • Where its energy goes: Glycolysis and Cellular Respiration.
  • Lab tests that depend on these structures: Activity 8 - IKI Test for Starch, Activity 9 - Benedict Test for Reducing Sugars.
  • Cellulose is why so much eaten biomass is lost as feces: Energy Pyramid and the 10 Percent Rule.
  • "Biomolecules: carbohydrates, protein, lipids" in Characteristics of life and in my Orgins of LIfe diagram.
  • Hydrogen bonding and polarity also explain Amphipathic Molecules and Membranes.

From my notebook

Check yourself

[!question]- What is removed when two glucose molecules join, and what is the bond called?
A water molecule is removed (dehydration synthesis). The bond is a glycosidic bond, a covalent C–O–C link.

[!question]- Why does iodine turn starch blue-black but not glucose?
Amylose coils into a helix that traps iodine. Glucose and maltose are too small to form a helix.

[!question]- Why is sucrose a non-reducing sugar?
Both of its reactive carbons are tied up in the glycosidic bond, so it has no free group that can reduce Benedict's reagent.

[!question]- What reaction turns starch back into sugar, and what does it need?
Hydrolysis. It needs water (and the enzyme amylase).
