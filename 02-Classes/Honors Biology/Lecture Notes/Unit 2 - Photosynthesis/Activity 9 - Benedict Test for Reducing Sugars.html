Activity 9 - Benedict Test for Reducing Sugars

← Unit 2 hub · Test 2 master notes · Class home

[!summary] Big idea
Benedict's solution detects reducing sugars. When heated with a reducing sugar it changes from blue to green, yellow, orange or brick red. Glucose and maltose are reducing sugars. Starch (amylose) and sucrose are not.

Method

  • Four test tubes, about two fingers deep: glucose, maltose, amylose, dH₂O.
  • 10 drops of Benedict's solution in each.
  • Heated in a water bath at 80 °C. (Benedict's needs heat; nothing happens cold.)
  • dH₂O is the negative control.

Results (Table 1: Benedict reaction observations at 80 °C)

Glucose Maltose Amylose dH₂O
Initial Light blue Light blue Light blue Light blue
Final Light orange Darker orange Yellow → greenish Still light blue
Result Positive Positive Very weak / almost negative Negative

Why

What "reducing sugar" means

  • Benedict's solution is blue because of copper ions, Cu²⁺.
  • A reducing sugar gives electrons to Cu²⁺, reducing it to Cu⁺, which forms an orange-red solid. The more reducing sugar, the further the color goes: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red.
  • The sugar itself is oxidized in the process. (OIL RIG: redox.)

Which sugars can do it

A sugar can reduce Benedict's only if it has a free reactive carbon (carbon 1) whose ring can open into a C=O group.

Sugar Free reactive carbon? Benedict's
Glucose Yes (the carbon 1 –OH; in α-glucose it points down) Positive
Maltose Yes, one: on the second glucose Positive
Sucrose No. Both reactive carbons are locked in the glycosidic bond: "hidden O", C–O–C instead of C=O Negative (expected; not tested in class)
Amylose Only one at the very end of a chain of hundreds Almost none: my tube went only yellow-green

[!warning] Wording fix from my notebook
I wrote "sucrose and amylose can not be reduced". Say it the other way round: they cannot reduce Benedict's reagent, so they are non-reducing. In this test the sugar does the reducing and is itself oxidized; it is the copper that gets reduced.

IKI vs. Benedict's: opposite patterns

IKI Benedict's
Detects Starch Reducing sugars
Positive color Blue-black Green → yellow → orange → red
Negative color Yellow-orange Blue
Needs heat? No Yes
Glucose − +
Maltose − +
Amylose + − (or very weak)

Using both together tells you whether a sample has sugar, starch, both or neither.

Connections

  • Structures of all four carbohydrates: Carbohydrates and Glycosidic Bonds.
  • The partner test: Activity 8 - IKI Test for Starch.
  • Redox vocabulary: ATP and NADPH.
  • Glucose is what the Calvin-Benson Cycle produces; starch is how the plant stores it.

From my notebook

Check yourself

[!question]- What color change shows a positive Benedict's test, and what does the test need?
Blue to green / yellow / orange / brick red. It needs heating.

[!question]- Why is sucrose non-reducing while maltose is reducing?
In sucrose both reactive carbons are used in the glycosidic bond. Maltose still has one free on its second glucose.

[!question]- A solution is negative for Benedict's and positive for IKI. What is in it?
Starch, and no reducing sugar.

[!question]- In a positive Benedict's test, what is oxidized and what is reduced?
The sugar is oxidized. The copper ion (Cu²⁺ → Cu⁺) is reduced.

[!question]- Why might amylose give a faint yellow-green instead of staying perfectly blue?
Each long chain has one reducing end, and a little starch may have broken down into smaller sugars, so there is a tiny amount of reducing group present.
